A Brief History of Personal Income Taxation

Origins

  • Income tax first introduced federally in Canada in 1917 to help finance WWI
    • Initially a temporary measure, but became permanent in 1949
  • Provinces began introducing income taxes earlier, in the late 1800s (BC, PEI)
    • Most others followed after the Great Depression
    • Newfoundland was last to introduce income tax in 1949
  • Currently, both federal and provincial governments levy personal income taxes
    • Authority comes from Constitution Act

Personal Income Tax Revenues

  • All governments, 2022: about 36% of tax revenue, not all revenue as in the table (OECD average 24%), and about 12% of GDP
  • Federal government, 2024–25: $234.3B, 45.9% of federal revenue and 7.6% of GDP

Table 17.1 covers all levels of government and runs to 2019. The two current lines use different measures, so keep them apart: the OECD figure is a share of total tax revenue for all governments in 2022 (Canada 6th highest of 38 countries), from OECD Revenue Statistics 2024. The federal figure is a share of all federal revenue in 2024–25, from the Fiscal Reference Tables 2025.

The book’s Table 17.2 compares personal income tax as a share of GDP in 2020: Canada 12.5%, the US 10.5%, the UK 9.5%, Australia 11.6% and Sweden 12.4%. Canada looks more like the countries with big welfare states than like those without.

Administration and Filing

  • The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) collects income tax for Ottawa and every province except Quebec
    • Provinces accept the federal definition of income; Quebec residents file two returns
  • Until 2000, provinces levied “tax on tax”: a percentage of federal tax owed
  • By 2001 all had moved to “tax on income”: each applies its own rates and credits to federal taxable income
  • The system relies on self-assessment: if you owe tax, you file by April 30
    • The self-employed file by June 15, but any balance is still due April 30
  • Many people who owe nothing file anyway to collect the CCB, the CWB and the Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit (formerly the GST/HST credit)
    • Even so, about 12% of working-age adults did not file (Robson and Schwartz, 2020)

Under the tax collection agreements the CRA collects provincial tax at no charge, as long as the province accepts the federal definition of income. Provinces can set their own credits, but the further those get from the federal ones, the more the CRA charges to run them. Quebec has run its own income tax since 1954. Example of tax on tax: federal tax of $5,000 and a provincial rate of 50% meant $2,500 of provincial tax.

The late-filing penalty only applies if you owe tax. The non-filer figure is from Robson and Schwartz (2020), using 2012 tax data: about 10–12% of Canadians, 12% of working-age adults. Non-filers miss out on benefits they are entitled to. Automatic filing for low-income people who owe no tax is still only a proposal (Bill C-31); it is not law.

iClicker Question

Under the old “tax on tax” system, a province’s income tax was 50% of the federal tax each person owed. Ottawa cuts everyone’s federal tax by one-tenth: someone who owed $10,000 now owes $9,000. What happens to that province’s income tax revenue?

A) Nothing, because the provincial rate is still 50%
B) It falls by one-tenth, without any decision by the province
C) It rises, because people have more after-tax income to spend
D) It falls only if the province decides to cut its own rates
E) The provincial rate falls from 50% to 45%, matching the federal cut

Answer: B. Provincial tax is half of federal tax, so it falls by the same one-tenth ($5,000 to $4,500 for this person) without the province deciding anything. The province loses half as many dollars as Ottawa ($500 against $1,000), but the same share of its revenue. E: the provincial rate stays at 50%; what shrank is the base it applies to. A ignores that the base shrank. D describes the system since 2000–01: provinces apply their own rates to federal taxable income, so a federal rate cut no longer changes provincial tax (a change to the federal definition of taxable income still does). C confuses tax revenue with spending.

Why “one-tenth” and dollars, not “10%”: a cut of 10 percentage points in federal rates would lower the provincial share of income by only 5 points, though provincial tax would still fall by the same proportion as federal tax. The old wording let “10%” be read either way, which made “falls by about 5%, half of the federal cut” defensible.

Computation of Personal Income Tax Liability

Calculating Taxes

  • Six steps: total income, net income, taxable income, federal tax, minus credits, then provincial tax
  • Not all income is taxed
    • Gifts and inheritances, lottery and gambling winnings, strike pay, legal settlements, First Nations income on reserve, gains on a principal residence, most refundable credits
  • Deductions come off total income before the rates apply, so taxable income is smaller than total income
  • Tax is withheld at source through the year
    • At filing you pay the difference or get a refund
  • The main return is the T1, plus schedules: see the CRA tax packages

Steps 1–3: From Total Income to Taxable Income

Step 1 adds up income from taxable sources (line 15000). Step 2 subtracts the first group of deductions, mostly pension contributions and costs of earning income, to get net income (line 23600). Net income is what most income-tested benefits use. Step 3 subtracts a second group, such as losses carried over and the northern residents deduction, to get taxable income (line 26000).

Steps 4–6: From Taxable Income to Tax Payable

Step 4 applies the rate schedule to taxable income. Step 5 subtracts the nonrefundable credits, most of them 14% of a set amount in 2026. Provincial tax repeats steps 4 and 5 with the province’s own rates and credits. Refundable credits come after all this: they are paid out even if no tax is owed.

The Haig-Simons Definition of Income

Haig-Simons Income

Haig-Simons (H-S) Definition of Income

The money value of the net increase in a person’s power to consume during a period: consumption plus the change in net worth

  • Includes all potential consumption, spent or saved, in any form
    • Employer pension contributions and insurance
    • Transfer payments: CPP/QPP benefits, EI, workers’ compensation, social assistance
    • Capital gains and losses: whether or not the asset is sold
    • Income in kind: employer perks, imputed rent on an owner-occupied home
    • Gifts and inheritances
  • Anything that reduces the power to consume is subtracted, e.g., costs of earning income

The Income Tax Act never defines income. It only lists sources: employment, business, investments including capital gains, support payments, pensions, EI and royalties. So economists use their own standard. Business example: revenue of $100,000 and expenses of $95,000 raise the owner’s power to consume by $5,000, so $5,000 is the income. Capital gains: a gain counts whether the shares are sold (realized) or held (accrued). Holding them is just choosing to save the gain, and H-S doesn’t care what you do with income. Income in kind examples: a company car, subsidized housing, free tuition for dependants of university employees. Imputed rent is what an owner could have charged a tenant, less maintenance and taxes.

Practical and Conceptual Problems

  • Business expenses: only income net of costs adds to the power to consume, but the line is hard to draw
    • A home-office desk that is also good furniture; a business lunch that is also a nice meal (50% of meal costs are deductible)
  • Capital gains: hard to measure until the asset is sold
    • Easy for traded shares; hard for art, wine, collectibles, crypto and NFTs
  • Imputed rent: homes and other durables yield services nobody pays for
    • Every house is different; cars and boats yield services too
  • Household production: valuable, but there’s no market price
    • Cooking, child care, home repairs, yard work

Evaluating Haig-Simons

Fairness

  • Horizontal equity needs every source of income in the base
    • Otherwise people with equal incomes pay different tax
  • But equal income need not mean equal well-being
    • Same income from pleasant brain work or unpleasant physical work

Efficiency

  • Taxing all income alike is neutral
    • Untaxed imputed rent pulls too much investment into housing
  • But neutral is not least distorting
    • Inelastic sources like land rent can be taxed more with no excess burden (Chapter 16)

Bottom Line

H-S is a benchmark, not a rule: judge each departure on its merits

Brains versus brawn: two people with the same income, one in pleasant office work and one in hard physical work in poor conditions. The first is better off, so is it fair to tax them the same? Land: the supply of unimproved land is perfectly inelastic, so a high tax on land rent causes no excess burden. McLure’s point is that optimal tax rules ignore how hard they are to administer and need a lot of information, so we shouldn’t throw H-S away either. The Carter Commission’s recommendations were built on H-S, and the 1971, 1981 and 1987 reforms drew on them.

iClicker Question

Under the Haig-Simons definition, which of these is not part of Sam’s income this year?

A) The rise in value of shares Sam bought last year and still owns
B) The rental value of the condo Sam owns and lives in
C) A $5,000 inheritance from Sam’s grandmother
D) The $10,000 Sam takes out of savings to buy a car
E) Sam’s employer’s contribution to Sam’s pension plan

Answer: D. Haig-Simons income is consumption plus the change in net worth. Spending $10,000 of savings on a car either swaps one asset for another (if the car counts as a durable) or raises consumption and lowers net worth by the same amount; either way it adds nothing: Sam’s power to consume didn’t change, only its form. Cash coming in is not the same thing as income. A, B, C and E are all Haig-Simons income. A and C are the tempting ones because Canada doesn’t tax them (gains are taxed only when realized, gifts and inheritances not at all), which is exactly the gap between H-S and the actual base.

How Canada’s Tax System Defines Income

Departures from Haig-Simons

  • Canada’s base is a hybrid of an income tax and a consumption tax
    • The Carter Commission (1966) and the 1971, 1981 and 1987 reforms moved it toward H-S
  • Some income is left out, or taxed only later
    • Left out: imputed rent, employer-paid health and dental plans, gifts and inheritances
    • Taxed later: employer pension contributions, when the pension is paid
  • Capital gains get special treatment
    • Only half of a gain is taxed, and only when the asset is sold (realized)
    • Capital losses can only offset capital gains
  • Exemptions remain
    • Gains on a principal residence are not taxed
    • Lifetime exemption on farm, fishing and small-business shares: $1,275,000 in 2026

Capital gains history: exempt before 1971; half taxed from the 1971 reform; a $500,000 lifetime exemption in 1985, cut to $100,000 in 1987 and ended for real estate in 1992 and for other assets in 1994. The share taxed went to two-thirds in 1988 and three-quarters in 1990, then back to half by 2000. The 2024 plan to raise it to two-thirds was cancelled, so 50% stands. Losses beyond gains can be carried back three years or forward indefinitely. The lifetime exemption was raised to $1.25 million in June 2024 and is indexed.

Realization: Gains Taxed at Sale

  • Buy an asset for $100; it grows 20% a year and is sold after 5 years
  • Half of a capital gain is taxable, at a tax rate of 50%
  • Realization (Canada): no tax until the asset is sold
Year Start of year Gain (20%) Taxable gain (half) Tax (50%) End of year
1 100.00 20.00 0.00 0.00 120.00
2 120.00 24.00 0.00 0.00 144.00
3 144.00 28.80 0.00 0.00 172.80
4 172.80 34.56 0.00 0.00 207.36
5 207.36 41.47 0.00 0.00 248.83
  • Sold in year 5: gain $148.83, taxable half $74.42, tax at 50% $37.21
  • Left after tax: 248.83 − 37.21 = $211.62

Nothing is taxed while the asset is held, so the whole value grows 20% every year. When it is sold, the gain is 248.83 − 100 = $148.83; half of that ($74.42) is taxable, and tax at 50% is $37.21.

Accrual: Gains Taxed Every Year

  • Same asset, but accrual (Haig-Simons) taxes each year’s gain that year, sold or not
  • The tax comes out of the investment, so each year starts from a smaller value
Year Start of year Gain (20%) Taxable gain (half) Tax (50%) End of year
1 100.00 20.00 10.00 5.00 115.00
2 115.00 23.00 11.50 5.75 132.25
3 132.25 26.45 13.23 6.62 152.08
4 152.08 30.42 15.21 7.61 174.89
5 174.89 34.98 17.49 8.75 201.12
  • Left after tax: $201.12, which is $10.50 less than under realization
  • Less tax in total ($33.73 vs $37.21), but only because the gains are smaller ($134.85 vs $148.83)

Each row: the gain is 20% of the start-of-year value, half of it is taxable, the tax is 50% of the taxable gain, and end of year = start + gain − tax. Year 2: 20% of $115 is $23; half is $11.50; tax is $5.75; $115 + $23 − $5.75 = $132.25. Amounts are rounded to the cent at each step, so every row checks by hand.

Accrual pays less tax and still ends up with less. The tax paid each year leaves less invested, so later gains are smaller. Under realization the unpaid tax stays invested and keeps earning 20% until the sale: the interest-free loan on the next slide. The $10.50 gap is what that deferral is worth.

The book’s version: $100,000 growing 12% a year for 20 years, taxed at 50% on half the gain. Taxed at sale, the owner’s net gain is $648,472; taxed as it accrues, $460,441, a difference of $188,031.

Deferral, Lock-In and Deemed Realization

  • Taxing gains only at sale defers the tax
    • The deferred tax is an interest-free loan from the government, so the asset grows at the pre-tax return
    • E.g., the $5 of tax not paid in year 1 earns $1 in year 2 ($0.75 after tax)
    • Hence the accountants’ line that taxes deferred are taxes saved

Lock-In Effect

Because selling triggers the tax, owners hold on to assets they would otherwise sell, so capital doesn’t flow to its best use

  • At death, gains are deemed realized: taxed as if the assets were sold
    • Unusual internationally; property left to a spouse rolls over tax-deferred

The example from the last two slides: under accrual the owner pays $5 at the end of year 1; under realization that $5 is owed but stays invested, and earns 20% ($1) in year 2. Half of the $1 is taxable at 50% when the asset is sold, so the owner keeps $0.75. Check: after year 2 the realization asset is worth $144 with $11 of tax owed on the $44 gain, so $133.00 net, against $132.25 under accrual. The unpaid tax keeps growing ($5, $11, $18.20, $26.84, then $37.21 paid at sale), and the earnings on it build the gap to $10.50 by year 5.

Many tax shelters are just ways of deferring tax. US studies of realization-based capital gains taxes do find a lock-in effect. Deemed realization means half the gains accrued over a lifetime are taxed in the year of death, so whatever the heirs inherit has already been taxed. That is one reason Canada has no inheritance tax.

Should Capital Gains Be Taxed Lightly?

  • Optimal tax theory supports a preference no more than H-S does
  • Arguments for a preference, and the doubts about each
    • Gains are windfalls, or reward waiting: but work involves sacrifice too
    • It spurs investment and risk-taking: not clearly, and there are cheaper ways to do it (Chapter 18)
    • It offsets inflation: true, but a lower rate is a crude way to adjust for it
    • Much of it is corporate income: the overall rate depends on corporate tax too (Chapter 21)
  • Verdict: measured against H-S, Canada’s treatment of capital gains is mixed

Mixed: there has been real movement toward H-S since 1971, but only half of realized gains is taxed, accrued gains go untaxed, and the principal residence, farm and small-business exemptions remain. On sacrifice: one could just as well argue that labour income deserves a preference because work is unpleasant, while capital gains only require waiting. There is no convincing way to rank which income takes more sacrifice.

iClicker Question

Ana and Ben each own shares with a $10,000 gain. Ben sells and buys almost identical shares; Ana keeps hers for 10 more years. Both face the same tax rate now and later, and the shares keep growing. Why does Ana end up better off?

A) Only half of Ana’s gain will be taxed, but all of Ben’s
B) Her gain is never taxed if she holds the shares until she dies
C) She defers the tax, which works like an interest-free loan that keeps earning a return
D) She doesn’t: in the end both pay tax on the same gains
E) Her gain becomes exempt once she has held the shares long enough

Answer: C. Ben pays tax on his gain now, so less of his money stays invested. Ana pays later, so the tax she will owe keeps earning a return in the meantime: an interest-free loan from the government. That’s why taxes deferred are taxes saved. D is the trap: paying the same tax later is worth more than paying it now. B forgets deemed realization: gains are taxed at death. A is wrong because both are taxed on half the gain. E: Canada has no holding-period exemption. The same advantage is what causes the lock-in effect.

Employer Benefits, Gifts and Inheritances

  • Employer contributions to registered pension plans are not taxed until the pension is paid
    • Nor is the investment income the fund earns along the way
    • H-S counts both as income when they accrue, but taxing them is seen as politically infeasible
    • Employer-paid health and dental plans are also left out
  • Gifts and inheritances are not taxed as income
    • Most industrialized countries tax them separately, through estate or gift taxes (the US among them)
    • Canada and Australia are the exceptions (Chapter 20)

Contributions are limited, but within the limits the pension plan is a tax deferral: no tax until money comes out, often at a lower rate in retirement. For gifts and inheritances, the fact that the donor paid tax on the money doesn’t matter under H-S, any more than a shopkeeper’s income is exempt because customers paid with after-tax dollars. Deemed realization at death partly fills the gap left by having no inheritance tax.

Tax Deductions and Tax Credits

Deductions and Credits

Tax Deduction

An amount subtracted from income to arrive at taxable income

Tax Credit

An amount subtracted from the tax owed to arrive at tax payable

  • Why have them?
    • Tax only net income: subtract the costs of earning it
    • Recognize unavoidable costs, such as subsistence and large medical bills
    • Encourage particular uses of income: saving for retirement, education, giving to charity
  • Deciding which costs are truly unavoidable is always controversial

Deductions Change Prices

  • Suppose spending on good \(Z\) is deductible and your marginal tax rate is \(t\)
    • Each $1 spent on \(Z\) cuts taxable income by $1 and saves \(t\) in tax
  • Example: \(Z\) costs $10 and \(t\) = 40%: each unit saves $4 in tax, so it really costs $6

Effective Price with a Deduction

A deduction lowers the price of \(Z\) from \(P_Z\) to \((1-t)P_Z\)

  • Two consequences
    • The relative price changes, so the quantity of \(Z\) bought changes too
    • The higher your marginal rate, the more a deduction is worth to you

Main Deductions

  • Child care: up to $8,000 per child under 7, $5,000 aged 7–16, $11,000 with a disability
    • Claimed by the lower-earning parent, up to two-thirds of their earned income
  • Union and professional dues, and employment expenses the employer doesn’t repay
  • Moving expenses when the move is to earn income
  • Carrying charges: interest on money borrowed to invest
  • Spousal support: deducted by the payer, taxed to the recipient
    • The payer is treated as passing the recipient’s income along
    • Child support under orders made after April 1997 is neither deducted nor taxed

All of these are costs of earning income, so deducting them gets closer to taxing net income: without child care, many parents couldn’t take paid work. The taxpayer has to be able to show the CRA the money was spent, and each deduction has rules to stop unreasonable claims. The child care limits are not indexed.

Interest Deductibility and Tax Arbitrage

  • Interest on consumer loans and on the mortgage on your own home is not deductible
    • Consumer interest is just the price of buying something sooner
    • Imputed rent isn’t taxed, so the interest that finances the house isn’t deducted
  • Interest on money borrowed to earn taxable income is deductible

Tax Arbitrage

Borrowing with deductible interest to invest in an asset whose return is untaxed or tax-preferred

  • Example: \(t\) = 40%, borrow at 10% (6% after tax), invest tax-free at 7.5%
  • Mostly helps high-income people, and is hard to police

In the example, keep borrowing as long as the gap lasts: every dollar costs 6 cents a year after tax and earns 7.5 cents. Limits in practice: there are only so many tax-free investments, and nobody can borrow without limit. The CRA watches for it; for example, interest on money borrowed to put into an RRSP is not deductible. But money is fungible, so it’s hard to prove what a loan was for. High-income people gain most because they face higher rates and borrow more easily, and a lot of effort goes into finding schemes and policing them.

  • Home Buyers’ Plan: borrow up to $60,000 from your RRSP for a first home, repaid over 15 years

RRSPs and RPPs defer tax: it pays most if your rate is high while you contribute and low when you withdraw. The TFSA (2009) is the reverse: no deduction going in, nothing taxed coming out, and nothing in it counts toward income-tested benefits such as OAS and the GIS. The government expected about half the TFSA benefit to go to seniors. RESP earnings and grants are taxed in the student’s hands, usually at a low rate. The FHSA (2023) is deductible going in and untaxed coming out if used for a first home. On saving: people may just move savings they already had into the plans, so the evidence on whether total saving rises is unclear.

Tax Credits Change Prices Too

Refundable vs Nonrefundable Credits

A refundable credit is paid out even if it exceeds the tax owed; a nonrefundable credit can only reduce tax to zero

  • A credit at rate \(x\) lowers the price of \(Z\) from \(P_Z\) to \((1-x)P_Z\), the same for everyone
  • A deduction’s value depends on your tax rate; a credit’s does not
    • A $100 credit is worth $100 to anyone who owes tax
    • A $200 deduction is worth $100 at a 50% rate, $60 at 30%
  • Most federal credits are set at the lowest rate: 14% in 2026
    • So a credit is worth the same as a deduction only for someone in the lowest bracket

Example of a percentage credit: with a 50% credit for research spending, $1,000 of R&D costs the taxpayer $500. The 14% rate follows the cut to the lowest federal rate (15% until mid-2025). A temporary top-up credit for 2025–2030 keeps 15% on credit amounts above $58,523, so people with large claims don’t lose from the rate cut.

Each costs little, which makes them a cheap way to be seen supporting something.

Charitable Giving: Credit vs Deduction

Canada: Credit (Ontario, 2026)

For gifts above $200 in a year:

  • Federal credit of 29% (33% on income in the top bracket) plus Ontario’s 11.16%
  • Each $1 given costs about $0.60 ($0.56 in the top bracket): much the same for every donor

United States: Deduction (Stylized)

A gift is deducted from taxable income:

  • The price of $1 given is \(1 - t\)
  • $0.76 in the 24% bracket, $0.63 in the 37% bracket
  • Richer donors pay a lower price
  • Canadian estimates put the price elasticity of giving above 1
    • Giving rises by more than the price falls, so charities gain more than the government loses

On the first $200, the Ontario credits are 14% federal plus 5.05% provincial, so a $100 gift costs $80.95. Above $200: 29% + 11.16% = 40.16%, a price of about $0.60, or about $0.56 for a donor with income in the top federal bracket. Donations qualify up to 75% of net income, and unused donations carry forward five years.

The US side is stylized. From 2026, US itemizers deduct gifts only above 0.5% of income, and people who don’t itemize can deduct up to $1,000.

If the price elasticity is zero, the credit is just a bonus for people who would give anyway. With a 40% price cut and an elasticity above 1, giving rises by more than 40%. Whether the government should subsidize gifts to private charities at all is a separate debate.

Credits or Deductions?

  • Before 1988, Canada used exemptions (deductions) for the taxpayer, spouse, dependants and age
    • A $5,000 exemption saved $2,500 at a 50% rate but $1,000 at 20%
  • The 1987 reform turned them into credits, worth the same dollar amount to everyone
  • But the choice is not that simple
    • Rates can be adjusted to offset any loss of progressivity
    • If the aim is to measure ability to pay, e.g., unavoidable medical costs, a deduction fits
    • If the aim is to change behaviour, it depends on who responds: a subsidy to someone who won’t change is wasted

A credit cuts the price by the same percentage for everyone; a deduction cuts it by more for people in higher brackets. If people’s responses differ, it can make sense to offer them different prices, so credits are not obviously more efficient. They are simpler and widely seen as fairer.

iClicker Question

Counting federal tax only: Ottawa replaces a deduction for some expense with a 14% nonrefundable credit on the same amount. Compared with the deduction, who loses?

A) Only people who owe no tax
B) People in the lowest tax bracket
C) No one, because a credit is worth the same to everyone
D) Everyone, because credits come off tax rather than income
E) People whose marginal tax rate is above 14%

Answer: E. A deduction is worth the taxpayer’s marginal rate times the amount; the credit is worth 14% of it. Above 14% the deduction was worth more, so those taxpayers lose. In the lowest bracket (14% in 2026) the two are worth the same, so B is wrong. A is wrong because a nonrefundable credit is worth nothing to someone who owes no tax, but so was the deduction. C mixes up “the same value for everyone” with “no one loses”. D confuses the mechanics. The stem counts federal tax only because a federal deduction also lowers provincial tax, since provinces tax federal taxable income.

For comparison, the book’s Table 17.3 (2017): total income $1.40T, taxable income $1.25T, nonrefundable credits $80B.

Tax Expenditures

Tax Expenditure

Revenue the government gives up through preferential tax treatment: exemptions, deductions, credits, deferrals and low rates

  • Exempting \(Z\) costs the same as handing $1 billion to the people who buy \(Z\)
    • One shows up on the revenue side of the budget, the other on the spending side
  • Finance Canada has published estimates since 1979 (2026 report)
    • Largest personal items, 2026: RPPs $25.4B, RRSPs $17.4B, partial inclusion of capital gains $16.8B, principal residence $8.5B
  • The items can’t simply be added up: they interact, and people respond

Same logic for credits: a $1 billion credit for charitable giving is a $1 billion grant to charities that happens to run through the tax system. Publishing the list, and the 1981 and 1987 reform papers, made people aware of that symmetry, so the preferences are no longer as hidden as they were. The 2026 report gives no grand total and warns against adding items up. Remove one break and people change what they do, so the revenue gained isn’t the estimate. A large total also doesn’t mean we’d be better off without them.

Tax Expenditures: For and Against

Criticisms

  • Estimates assume people wouldn’t change behaviour if a break were removed
  • They need a benchmark tax base, and there is no agreed one
    • One person’s loophole is another’s fair adjustment
  • They imply that untaxed income belongs to the government

Defences

  • Tax breaks are subsidies, so review them regularly like spending
  • They let us compare spending through the tax system with direct spending on the same goal
  • They allow benefit-cost analysis of tax breaks

The third criticism is philosophical: calling a tax break “spending” assumes the money was the government’s to begin with, when in a democracy property is private unless the public decides to transfer it. Defenders say the concept carries no such ideology. It is just a way of recognizing that the tax system is one of the main ways government subsidizes things, and rough estimates are still useful.

The Structure of Personal Tax Rates

Rates and Brackets

  • Canada has a progressive rate structure: higher incomes pay a larger share in tax
  • It uses brackets: ranges of taxable income, each with its own rate

Marginal vs Average Tax Rates

  • The marginal tax rate is the tax on an additional dollar of income: what matters for decisions to work, save and invest
  • The average tax rate is total tax divided by total income
  • The rate in a bracket applies only to the dollars that fall in it
  • Federal rates ran from 4% to 29% in 1917 and 15% to 84% by 1949; there are 5 federal brackets today

In 1917: a 4% normal tax on income over $1,500 (singles) or $3,000 (others), plus a supertax of 2% to 25%, for a top rate of 29% over eight brackets. War pushed rates to 15–84% by 1949. Since then rates have been compressed. Brackets: 15 by 1949, 10 after the 1981 reforms, 3 after 1987, later 4 and now 5. Simplicity was one of the five goals of the 1987 reform, but the Income Tax Act is still long enough to be called the accountants’ and lawyers’ full employment act.

Factors Affecting Marginal Rates

  • The statutory rate is the one in the law; what you keep from an extra dollar can be very different

Effective Marginal Tax Rate

The share of an extra dollar of earnings lost to income tax, payroll taxes and reduced benefits

  • Surtaxes: Ontario is now the only province with one
  • Payroll taxes: CPP 5.95% and EI 1.63% of earnings, up to annual maximums
  • Clawbacks: benefits shrink as income rises, which works like a tax
    • CCB, Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit, EI benefits, OAS
    • CWB, single person, 2026: 27% phase-in above $3,000 (up to $1,665), clawed back at 15% from $27,392 until gone at about $38,492

2026 payroll details: CPP is 5.95% of earnings up to $74,600, plus 4% (CPP2) up to $85,000; EI is 1.63% of insurable earnings up to $68,900. Employers pay matching amounts. Clawbacks: the CCB is reduced as adjusted family net income rises, at rates that depend on the number of children; OAS is clawed back at 15% of income above a threshold; regular EI benefits are clawed back at 30% for higher-income claimants. Each of these adds to the marginal rate over its income range. The CWB phase-in is a negative tax: each extra dollar earned brings 27 cents more benefit. A disability supplement adds up to $860.

  • Negative at low incomes while the CWB phases in
  • Over 50% in parts of the $30,000–$57,000 range, as the CWB, CCB and GST credit clawbacks stack on tax and payroll taxes
  • The rate does not rise smoothly with income

Jo is single, lives in Ontario and earns $30,000 in 2026. On an extra dollar of earnings, Jo pays 14% federal tax, 5.05% Ontario tax, 5.95% CPP and 1.63% EI, and loses 15 cents of the CWB. Counting only these, what is Jo’s effective marginal tax rate?

A) 19.05%
B) 26.63%
C) 34.05%
D) 41.63%
E) 14%

Answer: D. 14 + 5.05 + 5.95 + 1.63 + 15 = 41.63 cents of each extra dollar. Jo is in the CWB phase-out range ($27,392 to about $38,492 for a single person in 2026), so each extra dollar of income cuts the benefit by 15 cents, which works exactly like a tax. A counts only income tax, B leaves out the clawback, C leaves out payroll taxes, and E is the federal statutory rate alone. Jo is in the lowest bracket, yet the effective rate is not far below the top combined rate in Ontario (53.53%). In practice the CPP and EI credits trim this a little, and provincial credits that phase out can add more.

Alternative Minimum Tax

  • Deductions, credits and preferences can leave some high-income people paying little or no tax
    • E.g., large capital gains, dividend tax credits, tax shelters, big pension and RRSP contributions

Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT)

A second tax calculation on a broader base; you pay whichever is higher, regular tax or the AMT

  • In place since 1986, and tightened in 2024. For 2026:
    • Flat rate: 20.5% on adjusted income above an exemption of $181,440 (indexed)
    • Base: all of capital gains is included, and only half of most credits count

The book describes the AMT as the normal rates applied to a broader base, with a $40,000 exemption. That simplifies the pre-2024 law, which used a flat 15% rate. Since 2024 the rate is 20.5%, and the exemption is the start of the fourth federal bracket, $181,440 for 2026. Donations keep 80% of their credit rather than 50%. The AMT is a shadow tax system with its own base, aimed at people whose income is high relative to their regular tax.

Effective Versus Statutory Rates

  • Statutory rates can mislead about who really pays, for three reasons
    • Preferences: taxable income can be much smaller than a comprehensive measure of income
    • Incidence: taxes can be shifted, so the person who pays isn’t necessarily the person who bears the tax (Chapter 14)
    • Excess burden and compliance costs: taxes cost more than the revenue they raise (Chapter 15)
  • So statutory rates say little about how progressive the system really is
    • Lower rates on a broader base could be just as progressive, with less excess burden and less evasion

A tax-free benefit doesn’t let you escape the tax entirely. Say your marginal rate is 50% and your employer spends $500 on dental coverage that you value at $300. As wages, the $500 would leave you $250. You take the coverage, but you’d have preferred the cash, so the tax system still leaves you worse off. Similarly, when an investment is tax-favoured, its pre-tax return tends to fall by the value of the break, an implicit tax, so effective rates on high-income investors are higher than their tax bills suggest.

The Flat Tax

Flat Tax

One rate applied to everyone and to every kind of income, with no deductions except a personal exemption and strictly defined business expenses

  • For given revenue, the key trade-off is between the exemption and the rate
    • A bigger exemption makes average rates more progressive, but needs a higher rate
    • One rate of 25% (Alberta) to 30% (Quebec), with a $7,231 exemption, would have matched 2000 revenue (Emes and Clemens, 2001)

For

  • Less excess burden and less incentive to cheat
  • Simpler, so lower administration and compliance costs
  • Can stay progressive through the exemption

Against

  • May shift the burden from the rich to the middle class
  • Hard to confirm, since it depends on who really bears the tax
  • Canada moved partway: the top federal rate fell from 43% (1981) to 29% (1988) as the base broadened; a 33% top bracket was added in 2016

The 1981 and 1987 reforms are the moves toward a flat tax: the top federal rate went from 43% to 34% in 1982 and 29% in 1988, and the base was broadened by dropping deductions and taxing more interest, dividends, capital gains and fringe benefits. Alberta had a single 10% rate from 2001 until the NDP government added brackets in 2015–16. Recent reforms show a clear preference for progressive rates.

iClicker Question

A flat tax of 25% applies to income above a $20,000 exemption. What are the average tax rates for someone earning $40,000 and someone earning $100,000, and what kind of tax is this?

A) 25% and 25%: the tax is proportional
B) 12.5% and 20%: the tax is progressive
C) 12.5% and 20%: the tax is proportional, because there is only one marginal rate
D) 12.5% and 25%: the tax is progressive
E) 25% and 20%: the tax is regressive

Answer: B. At $40,000: 25% of $20,000 = $5,000, an average rate of 12.5%. At $100,000: 25% of $80,000 = $20,000, or 20%. The average rate rises with income, so the tax is progressive even though there is only one marginal rate. C is the misconception: progressivity is defined by average rates, not marginal ones. A treats the marginal rate as the average rate. D forgets the exemption for the higher earner. E divides the lower earner’s tax by taxable income instead of income. A bigger exemption makes the tax more progressive but needs a higher rate to raise the same revenue: the trade-off on the flat tax slide.

Choice of Tax Unit

Three Principles for the Tax Unit

  • Canada taxes the individual, and always has
    • Some countries tax the family: income is pooled and taxed on one return
  • Three principles most people find appealing

Three Principles

  1. The income tax should have increasing marginal tax rates
  2. Families with equal incomes should pay equal taxes
  3. Two people’s taxes should not change when they marry or move in together: the tax should be marriage neutral
  • With increasing marginal rates, no system can satisfy both 2 and 3

Principles 2 and 3 have broad support, though both are argued over. Support for increasing marginal rates is weaker, but they seem to have wide political support. Principle 2 holds other things equal, including family size.

The Marriage Penalty

  • Tax schedule: 10% on the first $6,000 of income, 50% above that

Family or Individual?

The case for the family

  • The Carter Commission (1966): the family is the basic economic unit
  • Families with equal incomes have equal ability to pay, so should pay equal tax
  • Taxing individuals invites income splitting: shifting income to a lower-taxed spouse or child
    • E.g., “sprinkling” income from a private corporation (CCPC) to family members, limited by the tax on split income (TOSI) since 2018

The case for the individual

  • Pooling taxes a second earner’s first dollar at the family’s top rate
    • Married women’s labour supply is more elastic, so this is inefficient (Chapter 18)
  • One-earner couples also get untaxed household production
  • Who counts as a family? Families don’t necessarily share income
  • Only the individual unit is marriage neutral

Income splitting is easiest with investment income: move the assets to a spouse or child in a lower bracket. The commission noted that with family taxation, transfers within the family have no tax consequences. Moving assets to a spouse just for tax reasons is risky given divorce rates, and there’s no strong evidence of large transfers. The 2015 Family Tax Cut let parents of children under 18 notionally shift up to $50,000 to the lower-earning spouse, with savings capped at $2,000; critics said it mostly helped high-income one-earner families and did nothing for single parents. The CCB replaced it in 2016. Pension income splitting is still allowed. On the family: the Carter Commission had the traditional nuclear family in mind; what about two sisters sharing an apartment, or a child supporting a parent? Canada keeps the individual unit and so gives up principle 2.

Taxes and Inflation

Inflation and an Unindexed Tax

Nominal vs Real Income

Nominal income is the number of dollars received; real income is what those dollars can buy

  • Tax law sets many dollar amounts: bracket limits, credit amounts, clawback thresholds
  • If they are fixed, inflation raises real tax burdens with no vote in Parliament
    • This happens even when inflation is fully anticipated

Indexation

Raising these amounts automatically with inflation, e.g., $10,000 becomes $10,200 when prices rise 2%

  • Canada indexes to the previous year’s CPI

Unanticipated inflation is usually seen as worse for efficiency, because people can’t adjust. But with an unindexed tax, even perfectly anticipated inflation changes real tax burdens.

Bracket Creep and Fixed Amounts

Bracket Creep

When incomes rise only with inflation, an unindexed progressive tax pushes people into higher brackets even though their real income hasn’t changed

  • Example: 10% on the first $6,000 of income, 50% above
    • Earn $6,000: tax is $600
    • Prices and income both rise 10%, to $6,600: tax is $600 + 0.5 × $600 = $900
    • Same real income, but tax rose from $600 to $900
  • Fixed credit and deduction amounts also lose real value
    • Child care limits are not indexed; the basic personal amount, age amount and medical threshold are

Even people who stay in the same bracket have more of their income taxed at their top rate. The child care limits ($8,000, $5,000 and $11,000) buy less child care every year they stay fixed.

Inflation and Capital Income

  • Even a proportional tax distorts capital income when there is inflation
    • Capital gains: an asset rises 5% while prices rise 5%; the real gain is zero, but tax is due on the 5%
    • Interest: tax applies to the whole nominal rate, including the part that just offsets inflation

Real After-Tax Return (Eq. 17.1)

\(r = (1-t)i - \pi\), where \(i\) is the nominal interest rate, \(t\) the tax rate and \(\pi\) inflation

  • With \(i\) = 6%, \(\pi\) = 3% and \(t\) = 50%: \(r = 0.5 \times 6\% - 3\% = 0\)
    • If inflation rises to 6% and \(i\) to 9%: \(r = 4.5\% - 6\% = -1.5\%\)
  • Borrowers deduct nominal interest, so inflation lowers their tax

Why proportional isn’t enough: if wages and prices both double, wage tax doubles and nothing real changes. Capital income is different, because the tax is on nominal gains and nominal interest. The book’s example: an asset bought for $5,000 and sold for $10,000 after prices double is a zero real gain, but half the $5,000 nominal gain is taxed. In the interest example, inflation and the nominal rate both rose 3 points, yet the real after-tax return fell. Inflation is not neutral even when fully anticipated, because tax falls on nominal, not real, interest. For borrowers it’s the reverse: they deduct interest payments that partly just compensate the lender for inflation.

iClicker Question

You lend money at a nominal interest rate of 8%. Inflation is 4% and your tax rate on interest income is 25%. What is your real after-tax rate of return?

A) 2%
B) 3%
C) 4%
D) 6%
E) It depends on whether the 4% inflation was anticipated

Answer: A. Eq. 17.1: r = (1 − t)i − π = 0.75 × 8% − 4% = 6% − 4% = 2%. B applies the tax to real interest only, (1 − t)(i − π) = 3%: what you’d get if the tax were indexed for inflation. C ignores the tax and D ignores inflation. E is the misconception: even fully anticipated inflation raises the effective tax, because the tax falls on nominal interest, including the 4 points that only make up for inflation. Here tax takes half the 4% real return, an effective rate of 50%, not 25%.

Indexation in Canada

Period Treatment
1949–1973 Not indexed: the CPI rose 72% from 1949 to 1971, and income tax went from 16% to 30% of government revenue
1974–1983 Full indexing of brackets and exemptions
1984–1985 Indexing capped at 6% and 5%
1986–1999 Only inflation above 3% a year; nothing after 1992, when inflation fell below 3%
2000 on Full indexing of brackets and credits restored
  • Capital income has never been indexed: measuring real gains is too complex
  • Some amounts are still fixed, e.g., child care limits and several credits (Hansen, Mescall and Purse, 2023)

A family of four with constant real income of $74,000 (2022 dollars) paid 12.7% of income in personal income tax in 1954 and 17.7% in 1970, with no change in statutory rates or brackets. That bracket creep was one reason for the Carter Commission. Inflation hit 8% in 1973, and the 1974 indexing followed. Nothing was done for interest, capital gains or other capital income, partly because indexing them properly is so complex: debtors would also have real gains to measure and tax. Hansen et al.’s table lists fully indexed items (brackets, basic amount, disability and caregiver credits, CPP and EI amounts, OAS, the CCB, the RRSP limit) and fixed ones (child care limits, RESP amounts, home accessibility and some other credits). It predates 2024, when the AMT exemption became indexed.

Should Taxes Be Indexed?

Against

  • Without indexing, governments must revisit the tax law regularly, a chance to fix other problems
  • Indexing slows revenue growth, which may push governments toward less equitable taxes

For

  • A stable, predictable tax law, with fewer chances for mischief
  • No tax increases without a vote: real rates shouldn’t rise by stealth
  • The stakes are large: by 1984, indexing had cut federal and provincial revenue by $19.5B, or 40%
  • Without indexing, low-income families lose most: fixed allowances and clawback thresholds hit them first

The strongest argument for indexing is democratic: the real tax schedule shouldn’t change through a process nobody votes on. Repeal would hit low-income families hardest. Families who now owe no tax would start owing as their nominal incomes grow, and clawbacks would start at lower real incomes unless the thresholds are indexed, as most are.

Treatment of International Income

Residence-Based Taxation

  • Canada taxes on residence: people resident here 183 days or more are taxed on their worldwide income
  • To avoid double taxation, there is a credit for foreign taxes paid, up to the Canadian tax on that income
    • E.g., Canadian tax on income earned in Germany is $7,000 and German tax was $5,500: pay $1,500 to the CRA
  • Canadians who live abroad owe no Canadian tax on their foreign income
  • The US taxes its citizens on worldwide income wherever they live, with a similar credit

Territorial vs Global Systems

A territorial system taxes citizens living abroad only in the host country; a global system taxes citizens on all their income wherever they live

Territorial vs Global Systems

Equity

  • A Canadian and an American live and work in Hong Kong with the same income
    • The Canadian pays only Hong Kong tax; the American may also owe US tax
  • Canada treats Hong Kong residents alike, but Canadian citizens differently depending on where they live
  • Should horizontal equity be defined by residence or citizenship? No system satisfies both

Efficiency

  • A global system can distort production: US firms abroad pay more for American staff, who owe US tax
  • A territorial system can distort location: Canadians can cut their tax by moving abroad
  • Which distortion costs more is unknown; the territorial system is easier to enforce

On production: other things equal, a US firm operating abroad pays more for its American employees, since they owe US tax on top of local tax, so a Canadian firm could win contracts even with the same technology. On location: Canadians can lower their tax by living abroad, while Americans can’t escape the IRS without giving up citizenship.

Summary

Summary

  • The personal income tax is the largest single source of tax revenue in Canada
  • Tax owed runs from total income, through deductions, to taxable income, then rates and credits
  • Haig-Simons income is the benchmark; Canada departs from it for capital gains, pensions, gifts and imputed rent
  • Deductions are worth more at higher rates, credits the same to all; both change prices
  • Effective marginal rates differ from statutory ones because of payroll taxes and clawbacks
  • No tax unit gives rising rates, equal tax for equal-income families and marriage neutrality; Canada taxes the individual
  • Without indexation, inflation raises real tax burdens; capital income is still taxed on nominal returns
  • Canada taxes residents on worldwide income; the US taxes citizens
